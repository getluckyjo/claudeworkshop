Briefing, not prompting

The single biggest difference between people who find this underwhelming and people who don't. A prompt asks for a thing. A brief says what you're trying to achieve and hands over what's needed to do it.

  1. The job. What you want done, and why it matters.
  2. The context. The brand, the constraints, the deadline.
  3. The material. The real file. Not a description of it.
  4. What good looks like. How you'd know it was done properly.
  5. The format. A spreadsheet, a page, a document, a file.

Working alone, the failure mode is politeness. In a room I'd tell you to reject a bad answer out loud and say exactly what's wrong with it. On your own it's tempting to quietly fix it yourself and move on — which teaches it nothing, and you pay for the same flaw again on the next one. Say what's wrong. It's faster.

A filter, not a bigger list

You can already find decision makers. Sales Navigator and Dripify do that part fine. What you have no filter for is whether the company behind the name is a big enough energy user — so qualification happens in the meeting, which is the most expensive place it could possibly happen. By the end of this you'll have a scoring system that reads a company and tells you go or no-go before you give up an afternoon.

The order is wrong, not the tooling

Nothing here replaces Sales Navigator or Dripify, and you shouldn't want it to. They find people, and they're good at it.

The fix is the sequence. Right now you find a person, then discover the company. Flip it: score the company on energy first, and only then go looking for who signs. Same tools, same campaigns — a filter in front of them.

What repeats afterwards

A skill that scores any company on request, so the judgement stops living only in your head — and a Monday morning list of newly qualified prospects, built while you sleep, with the reason each one scored where it did.

Watch for

Two traps. The first is scoring on industry alone — a food producer that outsourced its cold chain has the right SIC code and none of the load, so equipment and shift pattern beat sector every time. The second is forgetting that energy is only half the qualification: plenty of large users have a facilities manager who agrees warmly and can't sign anything. Authority was never your problem, so don't let fixing the energy half quietly lose it.

Two things to settle before you score anything

Both of these take minutes and both change every number that comes after. Neither is a technology question, which is why they tend to go unasked.

1  Your floor, as a rand figure

Your pitch is that you find more waste than the system costs. That sentence contains a minimum viable customer: monthly rental ÷ the waste you typically find ÷ what clients actually act on.

Rental R12,000, you find 15%, half gets acted on → the floor is R160,000 a month. Below that the deal can't work on its own logic and the meeting was always going to be a no. Until that's a number, “large energy user” is a feeling and every list is a guess. Your figures, not mine — but write it down.

2  The question that replaces the meeting

“Which Eskom tariff are you on — Megaflex, Miniflex, Nightsave — or does the municipality bill you as a large power user?”

Megaflex applies above 1 MVA of notified maximum demand, so “we're on Megaflex” is the qualification — nothing to estimate. People read it off the bill without feeling interrogated, because it's admin rather than commercial. Follow with “what's your NMD in kVA?” Anyone who answers that is worth a meeting whatever the list said.

And a company that doesn't know its own tariff has told you something too: nobody there is managing energy. That is the problem you sell into, so it's a lead, not a dead end.

The five steps

About three hours, or three sittings. Do them in order — step 1 is the one everything else is measured against, and it's the one you'll be tempted to skip because it isn't technical.

1  Work out your floor

“Here's my rental, the share of spend I typically find as recoverable waste, and roughly how much of it clients act on. What's the minimum monthly electricity spend where this still works for both of us? Show your working.”

Argue with the answer. Then ask what happens to the floor if you find 10% instead of 15%, or if only a third gets acted on. The range you get back is the honest one, and the top of it is the number to run the list against.

2  Teach it your judgement

“Here are three companies I know — one I'd love, one I'd walk away from, one I'm unsure about. Score each one and tell me why. Don't ask me first.”

This is the test that matters. If it ranks them the way you would, the model has your judgement in it and you can trust it on four hundred companies you've never heard of. Where it's wrong, say exactly why — that correction is worth more than the score.

3  Run it at your real list

“Here's my prospect list. Score every company, rank them, group them into priority, qualify, hold and decline, and give me one line on each decline saying why.”

The decline reasons are the point, not the leftovers. They're what stops the same dead company coming round again next quarter looking new. Expect the list to shrink a lot. That is the result, not a problem with it.

4  Put the filter in front of the funnel

“Rewrite my Dripify sequence so the Gap Analysis Report is gated on the tariff and the NMD rather than on a meeting. One sequence per industry, not one for everyone.”

Asking for the tariff in order to produce the GAR isn't a hurdle — it's obviously necessary information, so it reads as competence rather than screening. Do cold storage first; it's the clearest sector you sell into and the waste is easiest to name.

5  Make it repeat

“Turn all of that into a skill so I can score a company next month without re-explaining any of it. Then every Monday at 07:00, score anything new and send me three lines and what I should do about it.”

Three lines. A Monday brief that writes an essay gets ignored inside a fortnight, and then the habit is gone.

Then

One last thing, and check it with your accountant before you put it in front of anyone. Section 12L was due to lapse at the end of 2025 and Treasury extended it to 31 December 2030, at up to 95c per kWh saved, with measurement and verification by a SANAS-accredited body. Your system is the measurement layer those claims need — which turns a 60-month rental from a cost against savings into a cost against savings plus a deduction. That is a different conversation, and worth an hour with whoever does your tax.
