### What the room has in common

**Three of four are physical-product businesses with stock.** Two of them —
Danielle and Kowie — have effectively the same problem from opposite ends: data
about product movement that arrives faster than anyone can turn it into a
decision. Sit them together. When you put the four jobs on the whiteboard in
block 2, that overlap is the moment the room stops feeling like four separate
consulting sessions and starts feeling like a cohort.

**Only Danielle uses Claude daily.** The other three are weekly. Block 3
(briefing, not prompting) matters more than the run sheet assumes — three of four
have never written a real brief.

**Four seats, not eight, so you have double the per-person time.** Spend the
surplus on Danielle and Kowie; their builds are genuinely hard. Reni's will
finish early and Liezl's is undefined.

## Per-attendee prep

### Danielle — d distillery

**The problem with her brief: it's four projects.** Demand forecasting by
SKU, procurement against MOQs and lead times, product costing by channel,
project profitability, and rolling cashflow. Any one of those is a real piece of
work. All five is a quarter, not an afternoon.

**Build the project profitability model.** Reasons, in order:

1. It has a decision attached — *before committing to a large production run* —
   so it pays for itself the first time she uses it
2. It's self-contained. It needs assumptions, not a data pipeline
3. It demos the moment better than anything else in the room: change the payment
   terms, watch the funding requirement and the break-even sell-through move
4. Everything else she asked for either feeds it or falls out of it

**What it contains:** input and production cost per unit, selling price, gross
margin, payment terms in and out, funding required, interest cost if borrowed,
sell-through rate, and the three outputs she actually asked for — what she needs
to charge, how much she needs to sell, and how fast.

**Say this to her, early:** she named five models; she's leaving with the one
that makes the other four worth building. Otherwise she'll spend the session
worrying about the other four.

- **Artefact:** spreadsheet. Real formulas, her real cost lines.
- **Connector:** Xero if she's on it — confirm before the day. Otherwise Drive.
- **Skill:** `project-appraisal` — how she decides yes or no on a production run.
  She's the most likely in the room to actually use a process skill, because she
  already does this monthly in her head.
- **Schedule:** weekly rolling cash position with the committed production runs
  in it.
- **Watch for:** scope. She's the one who'll try to build all five. The model
  she leaves with should have five inputs she can change, not fifty.

### Kowie — Creative Beverages

**The cleanest, highest-value build in the room, and the one with a hard
dependency.** Four retailers, four export formats, all of them ugly. Without the
actual files there is no build.

**Chase the files before Saturday.** Not "bring your data" — specifically: last
week's stock-on-hand and sales-out exports from all four, as they arrive, not
tidied. If he only brings one, that's still a session; if he brings none, he's
watching someone else's build.

**What he leaves with:** a spreadsheet that takes one retailer's export and
produces the three views he named — stores out of stock, top performers, slow
sellers — plus a skill that repeats it for the other three. Four retailers in
35 minutes is not realistic. One retailer plus a repeatable process is.

- **Artefact:** spreadsheet, then a dashboard view if there's time.
- **Connector:** wherever the retailer files land. Gmail if they arrive by email
  — likely — which makes the Gmail connector genuinely load-bearing for him.
- **Skill:** `retailer-report` — the process, so retailer two through four is a
  ten-minute job rather than a rebuild.
- **Schedule:** Monday morning. **Be honest with him about the limit:** unless
  the files land somewhere Claude can reach, the schedule is a prompt waiting for
  him, not full automation. If they arrive by email, it's closer to real. Say
  which he's getting — an over-promise here is exactly the thing that sours a
  good session three weeks later.
- **Watch for:** him wanting all four retailers. One, done properly, then the
  skill.

### Reni — The Almond Girl

**The tidiest brief in the room and the one that will finish first.** Stockist
enquiries, price list and brochure, Meta ad product questions. All email, all
repetitive, all high-volume.

**The build:** a stockist page she can send as a link instead of attaching a
price list and a brochure to every enquiry. One URL, always current, opens on a
phone. Then a `stockist-reply` skill that drafts the response with the link in
it, in her voice.

That combination kills the whole job: the enquiry arrives, the draft is waiting,
the link is already in it, nothing is attached, nothing is out of date.

- **Artefact:** a page. This is the one genuine web build in the room, and it's
  also the demo everyone else should see — put her link on the projector.
- **Connector:** Gmail. Obvious, immediate, and the demo writes itself: *who
  enquired this week and hasn't had a reply?*
- **Skill:** `stockist-reply`, in her voice, with the pricing tiers in it.
- **Schedule:** daily 08:00 — new enquiries, drafts ready.
- **Watch for:** she'll be done by 02:20. Have the second build ready: a
  product FAQ page for the Meta ad questions, which is the same trick pointed at
  a different problem. Or pair her with Liezl.

### Liezl — Cape of Storms Apothecary

**The risk in the room.** *"Open to all tips, moving into a new business model
and target audience"* is not a job. It's an interest. Everyone else brought a
task with a file attached; she brought an openness.

**Phone her this week.** Ten minutes. Two questions: what's the new model, and
who's the new audience? That call is the difference between her having a great
session and her watching three other people have one.

**If you can't reach her, default to this:** a new model and a new audience is
itself a build — a positioning one-pager that says what she's now selling, to
whom, and why it's different. It's the highest-value thing an R&D person moving
into a commercial model can leave with, and it needs no data, no connector and
no files. It also happens to be the most forgiving build in the room if she
arrives with nothing.

- **Artefact:** positioning page or offer one-pager.
- **Connector:** Drive or Gmail, whichever she's on. Low stakes — hers is the
  station to keep short.
- **Skill:** voice, not process. She has no established process to capture; she
  has a new story to tell consistently.
- **Schedule:** the softest one in the room — a weekly prompt that asks her the
  three questions she should be answering while she works the new model out.
- **Watch for:** drift. Without a named job she'll happily explore for 35
  minutes and leave with nothing. Name the artefact for her in block 2 and hold
  her to it.
- **Admin:** her billing name (Cape of Storms Apothecary) differs from the
  business field (Liezl Kruger) and registration is N/A. Worth a check when you
  invoice.

### Where this session will actually wobble

**Block 7 is not 35 minutes for Kowie.** Messy retailer exports eat time —
merged headers, store codes that don't match, a different column order per
retailer. Give him the first ten minutes of your attention and let Reni run
ahead alone; hers is the one build that doesn't need you.

**Block 8 carries two stations in fifteen minutes.** Build each skill *from what
just worked in block 7* rather than starting fresh. If it's going badly, the
schedule matters more than the skill — the skill is easy to finish alone
afterwards, the schedule is the thing nobody does on their own.

**Danielle will out-run the room.** Daily user, five models in her head, and the
most technical brief of the four. Give her the project profitability model and a
second thing to start if she finishes: the costing model that feeds it.

**Liezl will under-run it.** See above. Phone her.

**The alcohol conversation.** Two distillers and a beverage GM in one room. If
Danielle and Kowie start comparing retailer terms and listing fees, that's a good
room — give it two minutes, then pull it back. It's also your best argument for
running an in-house or industry cohort later.
